# Right to record government officials in public

Reference notes only — see [`../README.md`](../README.md). Not evidence, not
timestamped, freely editable.

## Why this line of cases is in a MassTech Open Meeting Law repository

G.L. c. 30A § 20(f) gives any person the right to make a video or audio
recording of an **open session** of a public body, or to transmit it through
any medium, subject only to reasonable requirements as to sound, light, and
view that the chair imposes and states at the outset of the meeting.

The statute is not the source of that right — it codifies a floor that the
First Amendment already sets. The recording cases matter here in three ways:

1. **A public body cannot condition or chill recording** of officials
   performing public duties in a public place beyond what § 20(f) itself
   permits. Statutory permission narrower than the constitutional right is
   preempted; a body's internal policy narrower than § 20(f) is simply
   unlawful.
2. **Retaliation for recording is independently actionable** under § 1983,
   separate from any OML complaint to the Attorney General's Division of Open
   Government.
3. **The right extends past the meeting room** — to the sidewalk, the
   entrance, and the publicly visible operations of a government facility.
   That is the *Massimino* contribution.

## Controlling authority in Massachusetts (First Circuit)

| Case | Cite | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| *Glik v. Cunniffe* | 655 F.3d 78 (1st Cir. 2011) | First Amendment protects filming government officials in a public space; right **clearly established** in the First Circuit. Filming police on Boston Common. |
| *Gericke v. Begin* | 753 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2014) | Extends *Glik* to a traffic stop; the right is not forfeited because the encounter is a stop, absent a reasonable contemporaneous order. |
| *Project Veritas Action Fund v. Rollins* (consol. w/ *Martin v. Rollins*) | 982 F.3d 813 (1st Cir. 2020) | G.L. c. 272 § 99 cannot constitutionally reach **secret, nonconsensual audio recording of police discharging official duties in public**. Martin plaintiffs prevail; Project Veritas's broader facial challenge does not. |

Because the First Circuit crossed the clearly-established threshold in 2011,
qualified immunity is **not** available to a Massachusetts official who
retaliates against recording of public duties in a public place today. That
is the doctrinal difference between our posture and *Massimino*'s.
