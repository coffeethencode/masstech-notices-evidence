## Facts

- October 2018, Waterbury, Connecticut. Keith Massimino stood on a **public
  sidewalk** and video-recorded the **exterior of the Waterbury police
  station** — publicly visible areas, including police vehicles and the
  station entrance.
- Officers Matthew Benoit and Frank Laone detained him and **demanded
  identification**. He declined.
- He was arrested for **interfering with an officer**, Conn. Gen. Stat.
  § 53a-167a, and held several hours. He litigated the criminal charge for
  roughly three years; the Connecticut Superior Court **dismissed** it.
- He then sued Benoit and Laone under § 1983 (First Amendment retaliation;
  Fourth Amendment unlawful seizure/arrest; malicious prosecution).
  Represented by the **ACLU Foundation of Connecticut**.
- The district court granted the officers summary judgment on **qualified
  immunity** grounds on March 31, 2025 — the law was not sufficiently settled
  in 2018. ACLU-CT appealed April 28, 2025.

## Holdings

### 1. First Amendment — right recognized (merits prong reached)

The Second Circuit **held that the First Amendment protects the right to
record law enforcement activity in public**, and that the right reaches
recording the *exterior of a police building* from a *publicly accessible
sidewalk*.

> "a growing chorus of our sister circuits that have recognized a First
> Amendment right to record law enforcement activity in public" **[Q-SEC]**

> "That right encompasses Massimino's recording of the exterior of a police
> station from a public sidewalk." **[Q-SEC]**

Two features worth flagging:

- **Not a press privilege.** The holding does not turn on Massimino's press
  status. It is a right held by any member of the public — the same posture as
  *Glik*.
- **Recording a building, not an ongoing arrest.** The protected act was
  recording a police *facility* and its publicly visible surroundings, not an
  in-progress encounter. That is a meaningfully broader factual footprint than
  most of the sister-circuit cases, which arose from bystanders filming
  arrests.

The court took the merits prong of *Saucier/Pearson* first rather than
resolving on "not clearly established" alone — which is the whole point of the
decision: it makes the right **clearly established going forward** in CT, NY,
and VT even though this plaintiff loses.

### 2. Qualified immunity — officers immune anyway

The right was **not clearly established in the Second Circuit**
"at the time of the challenged conduct" **[Q-SEC]** (October 2018). The
out-of-circuit consensus was not enough. Officers immune on the First
Amendment claim.

### 3. Fourth Amendment — claims fail

- Officers had **reasonable suspicion** to briefly detain and question him.
- His **refusal to produce identification** then supplied **probable cause** to
  believe he violated Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53a-167a (interfering with an
  officer).
- That defeated the unlawful-detention, false-arrest, and
  malicious-prosecution claims.

This is the part that draws the criticism — a right announced in the same
breath as a rule that refusing to ID while exercising it supplies probable
cause. See Greenfield, *Tuesday Talk: Is There Really A Right To Record Police
In The Second Circuit?*, Simple Justice (Aug. 18, 2026).

## How this sits against the First Circuit line

For anyone already working the First Circuit cases, the relationship is:

- ***Glik v. Cunniffe***, 655 F.3d 78 (1st Cir. 2011) — the anchor. Filming
  police on Boston Common. Right to film government officials in public is
  clearly established in the First Circuit **as of 2011**. *Massimino* is the
  Second Circuit arriving at *Glik*'s merits holding fifteen years later, and
  is expressly downstream of it.
- ***Gericke v. Begin***, 753 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2014) — extends *Glik* to a
  traffic stop; the right is not forfeited merely because the encounter is a
  stop rather than a street arrest, absent a reasonable, contemporaneous order.
- ***Project Veritas Action Fund v. Rollins***, 982 F.3d 813 (1st Cir. 2020)
  (consolidated with ***Martin v. Rollins***) — Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 272, § 99
  cannot constitutionally be applied to **secret, nonconsensual audio
  recording of police discharging official duties in public**. Plaintiffs
  K. Eric Martin and René Pérez prevailed; Project Veritas's broader facial
  challenge to secret recording of other officials did not fare the same way.

**Key contrast for our purposes:** the First Circuit line has *already*
crossed the clearly-established threshold — a Massachusetts officer or public
official in 2026 has no *Glik*-style QI escape hatch. *Massimino* adds
**persuasive** weight (the Second Circuit is not binding in the First), and its
distinctive value is the **facility-exterior** fact pattern: recording a
government *building* and its publicly visible operations from a public
sidewalk, with no in-progress police encounter to record at all. That is the
fact pattern closest to recording a public body's premises and proceedings.

## Why this is in *this* repository

This repo documents MassTech's compliance with the Massachusetts Open Meeting
Law, G.L. c. 30A §§ 18–25. The recording cases are the constitutional floor
under the OML's own recording provision:

- **G.L. c. 30A § 20(f)** — any person may make a video or audio recording of
  an open session of a public body's meeting, or transmit it through any
  medium, subject only to reasonable requirements as to sound, light, and view
  imposed by the chair and stated at the meeting's outset.
- The *Glik* / *Gericke* / *Martin v. Rollins* line supplies the **First
  Amendment** overlay: the statutory permission is not the source of the right,
  and a public body cannot condition, chill, or retaliate against recording of
  officials performing public duties in a public place.
- *Massimino*'s facility-exterior holding is the persuasive extension covering
  the space *around* a public body's proceedings — the sidewalk, the entrance,
  the publicly visible operations — not just the meeting room.

Practical upshot: if a MassTech body or its counsel ever conditions recording
of an open session on anything beyond § 20(f)'s stated sound/light/view
requirements, the governing authority in the First Circuit is *Glik* and
*Martin v. Rollins*, with *Massimino* as the newest persuasive addition and a
clean illustration that courts keep expanding, not contracting, this right.
